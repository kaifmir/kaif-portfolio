Pay on Credit lets people settle rent, education fees and other big bills with their credit card, earning cashback on every transaction. 20,241 people a day started it, and it was losing 97 of every 100 of them.

What it does

Pay big bills with a credit card, and earn cashback while you're at it.

Rent, tuition and other large payments usually go out as cash or bank transfers. Pay on Credit puts them on a credit card instead. It competes head-on with CRED and CreQ, so trust and speed aren't optional.

My role

As Principal Product Designer, I owned it end to end: the diagnosis, the hypotheses and the shipped redesign.

I worked with PMs on priorities, and with data analysts to confirm which leaks were worth fixing first.

01 Diagnose

Where they left, and why

Funnel data showed where users left. A heuristic audit showed why. Together, they turned "conversion is low" into one question per moment.

Method 01

Funnel analysis

Mapped every step and measured drop-off at each one: a ranked list of where to focus, not a guess.

Where they leave
Method 02

Heuristic evaluation

Audited every screen against usability heuristics to explain the interface decisions behind each leak.

Why they leave
Moment 1 · The start · 48% drop

Is the landing page the main problem?

  • A 48% drop from landing to Step 1 suggests users can't understand the product's value.
  • Users arriving from Housing.com have a different intent; many are just exploring the feature.
  • A large share of incoming traffic has no real intent to pay.

Partly. A full revamp can wait; minor tweaks can win back people who have intent but don't see the value.

Moment 2 · The middle · 18% + 9% + 8% drop

Is the length of the form causing drop-offs?

  • User feedback: asking for amount, recipient, sender, PAN and card details in sequence is exhausting.
  • 51% prefer UPI, yet the flow defaulted to bank details and their extra manual fields.
  • Recipient and sender as separate steps make the form feel longer than it is.

Zooming in: the heuristic audit on three of these screens

Yes. Simplifying the steps is where retention will come from.

Moment 3 · The end · 14% drop

Is the savings experience rewarding enough?

  • Savings shown above the button and through coupons aren't celebratory enough to notice.
  • Users don't feel rewarded; the savings get lost in the technical details.
  • Nothing builds the positive reinforcement that gets people through the final payment.

No. Savings need to feel like a win, and show up long before the last screen.

Six hypotheses

From questions to bets

Each answer became a testable claim. Every change that follows tests one of these.

The start
H2

Show cashback value and consent as explicit moments, not passive banners → value registers, consent gets captured.

The middle
H1

Make the amount input the single focal point → more people finish Step 1.

H3

Move category-switching out of the header → it stops competing with the primary task.

H4

Replace the multi-step form with progressive disclosure → the form feels shorter.

H5

Default to UPI with inline verification → less effort, less doubt.

The end
H6

Make savings dynamic and celebratory throughout → users stay motivated to the end.

02 Design · Form-First

Focusing our energy where the friction was loudest

With limited bandwidth, a full redesign was off the table. So we put most of the effort into the form, kept the landing changes light, and wove savings through the whole journey.

A · Moment 1 · The start

A more intentional first impression

Tests H2

The leak: 48% left before Step 1, many unsure what the product was worth.

Surgical updates to build trust and show value before users face the form.

  1. Visible motivation. The 100% cashback offer moved to the top, so the reward lands before the task starts.
  2. Guided messaging. A generic header became conversational copy that helps users find their path faster.
  3. Subtle cleanup. A darker background and fewer offers cut visual noise, so key actions pop.

B · Moment 2 · The middle

Making the complex feel effortless

Tests H1, H3, H4, H5

The leak: 35% lost across nine screens, with bank details forced on a UPI-first audience.

  1. Amount input made the hero of the screen.
  2. Category switch demoted to an inline control.

Takeaways

What I'd take forward

Pair "where" with "why"

Funnel data ranked the leaks; the heuristic audit explained them. Neither alone would have pointed at the fix.

Diagnosis

Default to the majority

51% preferred UPI. Making it the default removed three manual fields for most people at once.

Effort

Show the reward early

Savings that appear from the first tap keep people moving; savings on the last screen are too late.

Motivation
What's nextThe landing page still deserves a full revamp. Much of its 48% bounce is low-intent traffic from Housing.com, which needs a different answer than a better form.
