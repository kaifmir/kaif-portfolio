Home buying: finding Housing’s right to win

Buyers could find homes on Housing.com, but got little help to compare, evaluate or decide. Research with buyers, brokers and developers surfaced seven gaps across the journey. We prioritised them by impact vs effort and shipped the quick wins first.

Why this research

Buyers can find homes on Housing.com. They get little help deciding.

We wanted to understand how buyers actually navigate the journey, where Housing adds value today, and which problems the platform should solve on purpose.

The objective

Separate problems that are perception-based from those that are structurally real, needs a platform solves best from those better handled offline, and areas where Housing has a defensible right to win.

My role

Research and design. I partnered with the research vendor to shape the study and the discussion guides, sat in on the moderated sessions and synthesised the findings into themes. I then designed the quick wins end to end, from the renter cross-sell and hotspots to the RERA trust signals, and took them to production with product and tech.

Where Housing stood

The current journey stages

Before solving anything, we mapped how buying actually happens today. Housing played in only two of nine stages. Everything before and after was an opportunity.

  1. 01Inventory communication

    Developers push inventory via brokers, ads and networks, with limited clarity

  2. 02Internal trigger

    Life-stage needs or investment discussions spark intent socially. Mostly FTBs

  3. 03External trigger / opportunity

    Informal networks, like laundry staff and security guards, shape early perception of areas and projects

  4. 04Lead generation

    Fragmented leads: FTBs via platforms and word of mouth, IBs via broker networks, databases and apps

  5. 05Understanding options

    Buyers explore across platforms, brokers and networks to decode scattered info. Mostly FTBs

  6. 06Shortlisting

    Options narrowed via brokers and other buyers, based on budget, location and value

  7. 07Evaluation

    Deep comparison via digital and physical site visits, discussions and trade-offs

  8. 08Validation

    Trust built via brokers, banks, family, physical verification, websites and videos

  9. 09Transaction

    Final closure happens offline, via broker or developer negotiation and paperwork

Stage 1Renting

Before they know they're buyers

Life events turn renters into buyers, and brokers reach them first.

#1

Engage renters before they are buyers

The Renter → FTB → IB evolution is the most structurally important insight in this research. Brokers exploit it from the rental stage, and Housing's rental inventory is an entry point other platforms don't have.

Where it breaks
  1. Buyers enter the journey before they know they're buyers, triggered by life events like marriage, kids or income growth.
  2. Brokers capture this transition early; platforms are absent until the buyer declares intent.
Defining factors
  • Rent-vs-buy decision toolEMI vs rent comparison with area-specific inputs
  • Area appreciation signals‘This area is growing; now is a good time to enter’
  • Readiness nudgesTriggered when renter behaviour suggests buying intent
  • Early relationship CRMLong-term engagement before purchase intent crystallises
  • Life-stage triggersMarriage, a child, a job change: signals that move renters

Stage 2Discovering

Searching and shortlisting

100+ listings and no help narrowing them down.

#2

Curated shortlisting beats discovery breadth

The platform surfaces 100+ listings and buyers collapse. A broker's core value is narrowing that to 2–4 intent-matched options, and Housing has the behavioural data to do this at scale.

What we derived

Discovery without intent-curation creates paralysis

Buyers aren't loyal to a locality, they're loyal to a budget. Given the choice, most would rather explore nearby and emerging areas at the same price than stretch for the exact neighbourhood.

68%
Quant survey · 140 respondents

of buyers across tier 1 and metro cities said they're more flexible on area than on budget.

The buyer"A home in Saket, around ₹2 Cr"
✓

✕
Saket, at ₹3 CrStretching the budget by 50% just to stay in one locality

This became the idea behind hotspots: curate the areas around what they asked for, within what they can spend.

Where it breaks
  1. Discovery volume without curation creates paralysis, not confidence.
  2. Buyers can't structure their own comparison: everything is tracked mentally, nothing is saved.
Defining factors
  • Intent signal captureSearch terms, revisit behaviour, time spent per listing
  • Budget & location fitStated vs inferred affordability, plus commute constraints
  • Family preference matchConfiguration, floor and amenity preferences across sessions
  • Comparison trackingSide-by-side saved listings with explicit pros and cons
  • Saved journey memorySession continuity, so no progress is lost on return visits

03Comparing hotspots Didn’t ship

Hotspots brought in leads and interest. But as a buyer, how do I tell which hotspot is right for me? I designed a side-by-side comparison of properties, ₹/sq.ft, YoY growth and search demand, so buyers could choose with evidence instead of guesswork.

Why it didn’t ship: a fair comparison needed real-time hotspot data based on each user’s history, which wasn’t possible from the data side.

The bet

Buyers are loyal to a budget, not a locality. Curated areas around what they searched beat more listings inside it.

Trade-offGoing beyond the stated locality could feel like we’re not listening.
How we handled itRelevant listings always come first; hotspots appear only after them.
Trade-offBorrowing marketing’s word “Hotspot” buys recall from the campaign, but means little to someone who missed it.
How we handled itThe module introduces the word on day 0, then leads with the benefit once it’s familiar.
Trade-offShowing ROI to home buyers adds a credibility risk: past growth isn’t a promise.
How we handled itROI is framed as three-year history, next to price, not as a forecast.

Stage 3Evaluating

Can I trust the listing?

The moment buyers leave the platform to verify a project on RERA.

#3

Trust is mis-timed, not missing

Buyers don't distrust Housing. They leave to assemble trust elsewhere, because the platform offers nothing at the critical evaluation moment.

Where it breaks
  1. Buyers can't verify the builder's intent.
  2. They can't validate their own decision, socially or financially.
Defining factors
  • Builder credibilityDelivery history, past project ratings, litigation record
  • Legal & RERA statusLive registration, approvals, litigation flags
  • Pricing fairnessFair-value benchmark vs comparable transacted prices
  • Resident validationVerified post-possession reviews from real buyers
  • Bank approval signalWhich banks have sanctioned loans for this project
What participants said
How we solved #3 · Trust

Bringing RERA, the trust factor, onto the listing

Buyers were leaving Housing to verify projects on the RERA website. We wanted that trust to sit right beside the listing, so RERA became its anchor.

InsightBuyers leave the platform to verify listings on RERA
Design moveA RERA tag that links to the RERA website, status sourced from RERA, and developer ratings
Result30% of users verify on RERA, with only 10% of them dropping the session
  1. 01
    RERA is why buyers leave

    Before committing, buyers left Housing to check the project on the RERA website. RERA is the factor they trust, so that is what we brought to the listing.

  2. 02
    More than a tag: a way to verify

    Instead of a static RERA badge, the tag opens that property’s page on the RERA website in a new tab, so buyers can verify it in one tap without hunting for it, or leaving Housing.

  3. 03
    Status, straight from RERA

    Every project status now comes directly from RERA, instead of the CRM team updating it by hand, so "ready to move" is verified, not just claimed.

  4. 04
    A rating for the developer

    Who's building it, with a star rating built from their track record: how many buyers they’ve delivered to, completion time against commitment, and their social score. It answers “will this builder deliver?” on the page itself.

  5. 05
    The quick fix, while the API was built

    Until RERA status was live, we showed when the property details were last updated, so buyers could judge freshness for themselves.

Sending buyers to RERA builds more trust than keeping them on the page.

Can I picture living there?

Separate from trust: buyers can’t feel a space from a spec sheet.

#4

Listings fail at conveying lived experience

Buyers make emotional decisions about spaces they can't yet inhabit, but listings are spec sheets. What buyers need takes developer cooperation or serious content investment to deliver authentically.

Where it breaks
  1. Listings cover specs, not experience: carpet area, not sunlight, noise or commute feel.
  2. The gap is only filled by a site visit, so the platform loses the buyer before validation.
Defining factors
  • Sunlight & orientationUnit-level east/west facing, floor, shadow patterns
  • Noise environmentTraffic, commercial and airport proximity indicators
  • Commute experiencePeak-hour travel time, not just map distance
  • Neighbourhood characterMarket, park and school density: walkability reality
  • Under-construction visualiserA realistic render of the final view, not brochure CGI

Filters, and how listings are shown

Every buyer is looking for something very different: morning sunlight, a quiet street, a short commute, a park for the kids. Search and discovery is one of the defining pillars of Housing, and lived experience could show up in two places in it.

Why we parked itHigh effort: it needs unit-level data on orientation, noise and commute, plus content partnerships with developers. Quick wins came first.

Stage 4Deciding

Doubt, restart, come back

A journey that loops for months, and used to forget everything each time.

#5

Buyers loop, not funnel: progress resets

First-time buyers cycle through search → shortlist → doubt → restart for months. Each restart erases prior progress, and the platform has no memory to help them pick up where they left off.

Where it breaks
  1. The journey is non-linear and long: buyers revisit projects, change preferences and restart.
  2. With no saved state, every session starts from zero and the buyer drifts to competitors or brokers.
Defining factors
  • Saved shortlist with historyA persistent shortlist across sessions, not wiped on logout
  • ‘You viewed this before’ nudgesReconnect returning buyers to prior shortlists and searches
  • Decision progress trackerShows where the buyer is in their journey
  • Preference drift detectionSurfaces when stated preferences change across sessions
  • Re-engagement triggersPrice drops, new inventory in saved areas, RERA updates
What participants said
How we solved #5 · Picking up where they left off

Remembering the properties buyers come back to

Buyers loop, not funnel, and every restart erases their progress. Now every buyer who dropped a lead sees a Contacted tag on that property when they return. Those back a year or more later also see how far its price has moved since they enquired.

InsightEvery restart erases progress
Design moveA Contacted tag for every returned user, plus the price increment for those back after a year or more
ResultRecently shipped: repeat leads are measured as users return after a year
  1. 01They returnA buyer who dropped a lead or contacted the seller comes back to the property
  2. 02We tag itThe property is matched to their lead and marked Contacted, for every returned user
  3. 03We check the gapIf it has been a year or more, today’s price is compared with the price when they enquired
  4. 04We show the changeThe increment tag, only for buyers back after a year or more, so the decision moves forward instead of restarting
The bet

Memory moves a decision forward: a buyer who sees what changed since their last visit restarts less often.

Trade-offUrgency vs pressure. Showing a year-old price creates FOMO, but could feel like a sales push.
How we handled itOnly for buyers who dropped a lead and return a year or more later, and only with the real price they saw: a fact about their own journey, not a countdown.
Trade-offWhen the price drops. Price since the last visit can go down as well as up, and a falling price works against the FOMO.
How we handled itIf the price has dropped, we don’t show the increment tag, but the property still carries its Contacted tag. Drops are rare in real estate, so this is the exception, not the rule.

The supply side and investors

Problems that sit outside the buyer's journey, but shape it.

#6

Lead quality is broken for the supply side

Brokers and developers both flagged platform leads as low-intent noise. The platform is seen as a top-of-funnel lead generator, not a conversion tool, and that erodes supply-side trust.

Where it breaks
  1. No intent signal travels with the lead, so serious buyers look the same as browsers.
  2. Spam follow-ups from the supply side damage the buyer experience and reinforce distrust.
Defining factors
  • Intent scoring modelA composite of search depth, revisit rate and enquiry timing
  • Hot / warm / cold segmentsTiered lead quality for brokers and developer sales
  • FTB vs IB classificationBuyer type, so leads are routed appropriately
  • Readiness signalsLoan pre-approval started, shortlist saved, site visit requested
  • Conversion likelihoodPredicted time-to-decision from historical behaviour
What participants said
Not solved yet · #6

Leads that arrive with context

An intent score and FTB vs IB classification, so brokers and developers can tell serious buyers from browsers.

Why we parked itIt needs an intent-scoring model and supply-side buy-in on new lead tiers: a large, cross-team effort.

#7

Investor buyers are unreached, broker-only right now

Investor buyers never use open platforms. They access deals through trusted broker networks, NRI databases and past-client relationships, which needs a fundamentally different product mode.

Where it breaks
  1. Open platforms aren't part of the IB journey: discovery, shortlisting and closure all happen through broker networks.
  2. Their criteria are different: ROI, appreciation and resale, not livability.
Defining factors
  • ROI & yield calculatorGross yield, appreciation potential, rental cover of EMI
  • Micro-market appreciationLocality-level price trend and infrastructure pipeline
  • Exclusive inventory accessPre-launch and broker-curated, not on the open platform
  • Resale potential signalsHistorical price movement for the project or micro-market
  • Network & NRI accessReferral-based onboarding and a trusted community layer
What participants said
Not solved yet · #7

A distinct mode for investors

ROI and yield tools, micro-market trends and curated, broker-led inventory for buyers who never use open platforms.

Why we parked itInvestors need a different product mode, not a feature. It sits outside the buyer-first focus of this work.

How we prioritised

Impact vs effort: the low-hanging fruit first

Every theme went into the cross-functional workshop as a bet, scored on its impact for buyers and the platform against the effort to build it. We solved the low-hanging fruit first, and set a clear direction for the bigger bets.

  • #1 Rental inventoryAn entry point to future buyers that other platforms don’t have
  • #2 Behavioural dataSearch and budget history to curate options at scale, like a broker would
  • #3 RERA on the listingVerification at the moment of evaluation, instead of off-platform

Reflection

What I’d do differently

The work moved leads and earned trust. Looking back critically, here is where the approach fell short, and what I’d change.

Leads are a proxy, not the outcome.

We measured lifts in leads against production. The research’s core problem was deciding, and a lead doesn’t tell us whether a buyer decided faster or with more confidence.

Next timeDefine success before building: site visits, repeat contacts and time-to-decision, with a clear definition of a high-intent lead.

Quick wins favoured the top of the funnel.

Impact vs effort put discovery and trust signals first. The deepest finding, lived experience (#4), is still open, and #5 shipped as one slice of a much bigger looping problem.

Next timePair every quick win with one long bet funded in parallel, so the hard problems don’t wait for a quiet quarter.

Effort was scored before data feasibility.

The hotspot comparison was designed before we knew the data side couldn’t support it in real time. Its effort score was wrong because the dependency was invisible.

Next timeBring data and engineering into the scoring, and treat data availability as its own axis in prioritisation.

Impact scores leaned on qualitative evidence.

The 68% quant survey ran after the qual themes were set, and validated only one of them. The other themes were scored on interviews and workshop judgement.

Next timeSize every theme quantitatively before the workshop, so impact is argued with numbers, not conviction.
